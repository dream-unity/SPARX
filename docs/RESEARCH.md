# Beacon Isles: SPARX research and design provenance

Research checked: **25 October 2026**. Repository: **SPARX**. Playable project: **Beacon Isles**.

Beacon Isles is an independent educational adventure informed by published descriptions of SPARX and general CBT skill topics. It is not the University of Auckland programme, an authorised SPARX release, a clinical assessment, or a treatment demonstrated effective in a trial. Its new code, writing, characters and presentation must not be represented as the intervention studied in the research below.

## Scope and method

This is a focused primary-source research review supporting the first implementation, not a registered systematic review or meta-analysis. Sources were sought through the official SPARX publication list, original research articles, institutional author repositories and a direct developer interview. The review deliberately includes positive findings, null findings, implementation experience and participatory design evidence. It separates four questions:

1. What did the original programme teach and ask players to do?
2. What did particular studies establish in particular populations?
3. What can reasonably inform the design of an independent game?
4. What still requires evaluation of this game itself?

Publisher access was inconsistent. The 2012 paper was read through its author-hosted full PDF. For the 2025 Future Proofing trial, the publisher's indexed text and institutional abstract were available although direct publisher/PDF opens returned access errors. Findings below identify their sources and preserve study denominators and outcome time points. No account was created in the official game, and this review is not a complete playthrough or extraction of its source code.

## Original programme: what is being learned from

SPARX stands for Smart, Positive, Active, Realistic, X-factor thoughts. Published developer research describes a three-dimensional fantasy adventure with a customizable avatar, seven provinces, characters, puzzles and minigames. A guide introduces each session and helps connect the adventure back to everyday situations. The original design used participatory input from young people, clinicians and cultural advisors. Māori youth and family consultations specifically examined relevance and possible family involvement. These studies concern the design and acceptability of the original programme; they do not establish that any fantasy skin around CBT content works equally well. [S2]

In a direct developer interview, Mathijs Lucassen explains that the original game demanded little game-skill mastery and largely avoided losing or dying. He describes the difficulty of balancing explicit instruction with engaging play: overly implicit messages may be missed, while simplified instruction may feel patronising. Story and avatar-based exploration coexist with guide-led explanation and real-life practice. These are useful design tensions, rather than a validated formula for a new game. [S3]

**Interpretation for this project:** completing an adventure level is evidence of completing a programmed activity. It is not evidence of recovery, emotional maturity, general intelligence, or mastery of everyday psychological problems. Scenario performance is similarly limited to the tasks actually attempted.

## Seven published skill stages and the ten-level adaptation

The historical topic column is a concise paraphrase of the original paper's module table. The Beacon Isles names and activities are this project's own design. The mapping is conceptual, not a reproduction of the original scripts, puzzles, therapeutic dose or programme. [S1]

| Level | Original SPARX province and topic | Beacon Isles level | Intended learning activity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Cave: hope, CBT introduction, breathing | Haven of Light | Separate an event, interpretation, feeling and response; try a comfortable breathing or grounding pause. |
| 2 | Ice: activation, muscle relaxation, communication | Frostfall Garden | Choose a manageable activity and a practical next step. |
| 3 | Volcano: strong emotions, assertiveness, listening | Ember Hollow | Acknowledge emotion while choosing an assertive response. |
| 4 | Mountain: problem-solving and helpful thinking | Cloudstep Ridge | Define a problem, compare options and select a workable action. |
| 5 | Swamp: recognising unhelpful thinking patterns | Mistwood Marsh | Identify reasoning habits without treating all unpleasant thoughts as errors. |
| 6 | Bridgeland: examining thoughts and negotiation | Bridge of Perspective | Weigh evidence and construct a balanced, actionable interpretation. |
| 7 | Canyon: integration, distress tolerance, setbacks | Stillwater Canyon | Combine earlier skills and identify when support or a different approach is needed. |
| 8 | **Original extension; no corresponding original level** | Observatory of Assumptions | Distinguish observations, assumptions and uncertainty in more ambiguous situations. |
| 9 | **Original extension; no corresponding original level** | Experiment Workshop | Design a small, safe behavioural experiment with a prediction and an observable outcome. |
| 10 | **Original extension; no corresponding original level** | Beacon Summit | Apply several skills to unfamiliar scenarios and form a practical continuation plan. |

Levels 8–10 extend the educational sequence; no cited study tests this sequence or demonstrates that ten levels are better than seven. The current content module is authoritative for actual playable prompts. This table specifies intended learning coverage, not a claim that all original SPARX content has been reproduced.

## Evidence ledger

| Study and context | Population and comparison | Findings and important limits |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Merry et al., 2012 — treatment trial** [S1] | 187 help-seeking adolescents, 12–19, across 24 New Zealand sites; SPARX versus usual care. | Non-inferiority supported. Per-protocol CDRS-R change: 10.32 versus 7.59; difference 2.73, 95% CI −0.31–5.77. Secondary remission: 43.7% versus 26.4%. Benefits persisted at three-month follow-up. High-risk/severe cases were excluded. Among 80 adherence respondents, 86% completed four or more modules; 60% completed seven. These were self-reports. |
| **Poppelaars et al., 2016 — indicated school prevention** [S4] | 208 Dutch girls with elevated symptoms; SPARX, group CBT, their combination, and monitoring. | Symptoms decreased across conditions; no between-condition benefit was detected. Findings concern this population and delivery setting. The result cautions against assuming that clinical results transfer unchanged to school prevention. |
| **Perry et al., 2017 — prevention before final examinations** [S5] | 540 final-year students in ten Sydney schools; school-cluster randomisation to SPARX-R or an attention-matched control. | Lower depressive symptoms after intervention (d=.29) and six months after baseline (d=.21); no statistically significant difference at 18 months. This was a prevention adaptation and older school population, not a replication of treatment in help-seeking clinical users. |
| **Werner-Seidler et al., 2025 — scaled universal prevention** [S6] | 6,388 students in 134 Australian schools; smartphone programme versus control; schools randomised. | No difference in depressive-symptom change at the 12-month primary endpoint: −0.05, 95% CI −0.36–0.23, p=.75. No secondary-outcome advantage. Of those allocated the programme, 43% completed zero modules, 22% completed at least four and 12% completed seven. Many schools did not supply intended class time. Low engagement may help explain delivery problems; it does not justify ignoring the null randomised result or assuming benefit among completers. |
| **Fleming et al., 2025 — national observational implementation** [S7] | New Zealand registrants aged 12–19, April 2014–September 2021; 21,320 registered and 13,564 started. No comparison group. | Of starters, 51.1% completed module 1, 7.4% at least four and 3.1% all seven. Repeated-assessment users showed lower PHQ-A symptoms later. Selection, spontaneous change and missing outcomes prevent causal attribution. Authors identify device/download barriers, navigation friction and save-point opportunities. These data predate the later SPARX iteration. |

### How to read these findings together

The studies ask different questions. A help-seeking treatment sample, an indicated prevention sample, an older class approaching exams and a younger universal school population are not interchangeable. Nor are a trial's supported delivery conditions and an unguided public website. A prevention null result does not erase a treatment result, but a treatment result cannot be used as proof of successful universal prevention.

Non-inferiority is also not a finding that two interventions are identical or that the game is superior to every form of therapist-delivered CBT. The comparator, population, outcome and study design matter. A favourable secondary outcome should not replace the primary comparison in a headline.

Completion statistics require explicit denominators. Registration, opening the programme, finishing one module, finishing four modules and finishing the entire programme are different events. The same word, “completion,” has been used for different thresholds across publications. Beacon Isles should define its own events plainly and keep them separate from psychological outcomes.

Observational change among people who remain engaged cannot establish what would have happened to them without the programme. Equally, comparing self-selected high engagers with low engagers does not recreate random assignment. Motivation, baseline circumstances and other support may differ.

**Project conclusion:** the literature motivates careful experimentation with an accessible skills adventure. It does not supply efficacy, clinical safety, dose-response, transfer or superiority evidence for Beacon Isles.

## Engagement, language and inclusion

Fleming et al.'s 2019 focus-group study exposed 79 adolescents to introductions for SPARX and SPARX-R. Participants often preferred broadly relevant skills language; some preferred directly acknowledging depression. Choice was a strong theme. Speed of access mattered, and some considered the presentation dated. This is qualitative and preference evidence, not a trial demonstrating that changing the wording improves mental health. [S8]

The original cultural-development research is a reason to involve intended users, not a licence to transplant cultural symbols into an unrelated setting. Beacon Isles uses original fictional locations and should not imply that it has received the cultural consultation, youth co-design or clinical review undertaken for SPARX. [S2]

The official usage guidance encourages spacing sessions and practising between them. Its approximately 30-minute sessions and one-to-two-per-week guidance describe that programme. They are not a dosage established for this shorter new game. [S9]

## Research-to-design decisions

The following are implementation choices and testable design hypotheses. They should not be presented as findings that the research has already established for this project.

| Design concern | Beacon Isles response | What still needs to be tested |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Getting from explanation to use | A scenario or activity follows an explanation; closing prompts invite one manageable everyday action. | Whether users understand the concept and can apply it outside the displayed examples. |
| Confusing game progress with recovery | Describe rewards as beacons, activities and adventure progress. Make no diagnostic interpretation of progress. | Whether users understand that the game is educational and that finishing is not a health assessment. |
| Confusing supportive teaching with forced positivity | Prefer alternatives proportionate to evidence. Allow uncertainty, valid disappointment, boundaries and practical support. | Whether feedback accidentally invalidates a reasonable concern or treats a harmful situation as merely a thinking problem. |
| Challenge that becomes discouragement | Use guidance and retries. Increase contextual ambiguity and combination of skills rather than reaction speed. | Whether challenge is interesting without becoming shaming, confusing or repetitive. |
| Transfer beyond answer recognition | The original extensions introduce ambiguous assumptions, experiments and mixed scenarios. | Whether learning generalises to unfamiliar questions and persists after a delay. |
| Mobile and navigation friction | Keep controls simple and readable; support touch and keyboard; avoid unnecessary loading or account barriers. | Completion of the complete flow on representative phones and assistive technology, not just desktop layout. |
| Losing work or momentum | Use small progress checkpoints and clear resume behaviour. | Recovery after reload, navigation, browser storage failure and interrupted sessions. |
| Sensitive personal writing | Make personal reflection optional. Keep it separate from public content and provide clear local-data handling. | Whether persistence, deletion and any export match the interface's promises. |
| Breathing activities excluding some players | Invite comfortable normal breathing, allow stopping, and offer an alternative grounding activity. | Whether the activity remains understandable and accessible without animation or breath pacing. |
| Visual accessibility | Avoid flashing effects; offer reduced motion, visible focus, readable text and sufficient contrast. | Keyboard-only completion, text enlargement, reduced-motion behaviour and screen-reader interaction. |
| Measuring engagement responsibly | Treat repeat visits, task attempts and completion as product outcomes. | Whether improved retention reflects useful learning rather than pressure to remain in the game. |

## Boundaries and deliberate omissions

Beacon Isles must not claim to offer the official SPARX treatment, diagnose depression, provide crisis monitoring, or determine whether a player is safe. It must not claim that an answer choice proves anything about that player's real-life personality or competence.

The initial educational scope does not require administering PHQ-A, CDRS-R or another clinical instrument. A symptom questionnaire would create different expectations concerning interpretation, clinical oversight, safeguarding and privacy. Adding one should be a separate, reviewed decision rather than an incidental game mechanic.

The project does not reproduce original game assets, voice acting, dialogue, cultural imagery, characters, clinical questionnaires or proprietary source code. No independent clinical or cultural validation is implied by citing the original research. No study reviewed here establishes that this implementation's shorter interactions reproduce SPARX's intervention intensity.

A support entry should clearly say what help the game does and does not provide, without asking players to disclose sensitive circumstances in order to continue. Support resources should be appropriate to location and checked before publication. The game should not imply that someone monitors local notes or gameplay responses.

## Rights, naming and current availability

The official terms, updated June 2026, state that SPARX content, software, design and trademarks are owned by or licensed to the University of Auckland, restrict reproduction/adaptation and describe SPARX as a registered trademark. This report records the published terms rather than making an independent trademark-register determination. [S10]

Consequently, this repository retains the user-requested repository identifier while the playable experience uses the independent title **Beacon Isles**. Research attribution identifies the programme being discussed; it does not claim affiliation or permission. Any later use of official assets or branding would require a separate rights determination.

On the research date, the official homepage displayed a temporary technical-issues notice. This is a point-in-time observation, not evidence of permanent discontinuation or of the availability of any particular downloadable version. [S11]

## Evaluation roadmap

This is a proposed development sequence, not research already completed.

### 1. Verify the software and the learning content separately

Software checks should establish that each level is reachable in the intended order, branching and retries work, progress survives reload, reset behaves as stated, and controls remain usable on mobile and keyboard. Content review should independently examine answer keys, feedback, ambiguous scenarios, tone and the distinction between interpretation and observable evidence. Passing one type of review does not substitute for the other.

### 2. Run usability sessions with intended users

Observe people completing activities without coaching. Record where instructions are misunderstood, where navigation interrupts attention and where a response seems patronising or invalidating. Include a range of reading levels, devices and access needs. Measure task success and misunderstanding rather than using “time spent” as a proxy for value. Participation and data collection should be proportionate, transparent and consensual.

### 3. Test learning with unfamiliar material

Use independently written pre/post and delayed questions that are not simply repetitions of the game's answer choices. Assess whether users distinguish observations from assumptions, compare options and formulate balanced responses in new contexts. Report retention and uncertainty; do not relabel a small quiz gain as improved mental health. Scoring criteria should allow more than one defensible response where appropriate.

### 4. Evaluate real-world transfer before asserting it

Ask whether participants can describe and undertake a chosen action outside the game, what prevents that action and whether the skill remained useful in a different situation. Recognise that self-report and selective follow-up have limits. Do not claim that game scores alone establish behavioural change.

### 5. Treat clinical evaluation as a distinct project

Any future claim to reduce symptoms or prevent illness would need an appropriately governed clinical study of the actual version being offered, with a prespecified population, comparator, primary outcome and follow-up. It would also need suitable expertise, consent arrangements, risk procedures, adverse-event handling and a realistic delivery model. The scientific question should distinguish the contribution of game content from attention, expectations, ordinary improvement and other support.

Until such work exists, the accurate description remains **an educational CBT skills adventure informed by published research**.
